Therapist: Hi Boyd, come on in. Last session we set up a behavioral experiment. You were going to start one conversation with a coworker in the break room. Did you get to try it?
Client: I did, actually. Wednesday. I asked Dave about his weekend.
Therapist: How did it go?
Client: Fine? He talked for like five minutes about his kid's soccer thing. I mostly just nodded but I didn't die.
Therapist: Before you did it, what was the prediction? What did you think would happen?
Client: That I'd freeze, say something stupid, and he'd think I was weird and tell people.
Therapist: And what actually happened?
Client: None of that. He seemed happy someone asked. I don't think he thought about it at all after.
Therapist: So let's hold those two things next to each other. The prediction was public humiliation and lasting judgment. The evidence was a normal, forgettable, even pleasant exchange. What do you make of that gap?
Client: That my brain is writing checks the situation doesn't cash.
Therapist: I love that phrasing. This is the cognitive piece. The anxious prediction feels like fact, but when we run the experiment, the data keeps disagreeing with it. One data point doesn't rewrite years of belief, but it's a crack in it.
Client: It did feel a little easier the second time I saw him.
Therapist: That's the mechanism. Avoidance keeps the fear frozen. Approach lets it update. For this week I'd like to raise it one notch. Not just respond, but initiate and ask one follow-up question. Same idea, slightly more exposure.
Client: That's harder but okay. I can try that.
Therapist: Good. Jot down your prediction beforehand and what actually happened after, so we can compare again. You're doing the work, and it's showing.
Client: Thanks. See you next week.
